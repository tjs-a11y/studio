IRS Finalizes Rules Letting It Take Card Tax Payments Directly

Final IRS card-payment rules, a proposed premium tax credit overhaul, Ohio use tax on untaxed wine orders, and retirement distribution reporting.

The IRS has issued final rules under the Taxpayer First Act that let it take credit and debit card tax payments itself, without a third-party processor.

IRS can now take cards directly

  • Final regulations let the IRS accept credit and debit card tax payments itself, cutting out the third-party processor step.
  • The rules carry out Taxpayer First Act changes, so how businesses pay by card could shift.
  • Restrictions on the IRS paying fees under card-payment contracts are removed, which could change who bears processing costs.

Source: Federal Register: IRS: Rule: Regulations Regarding Payment of Tax by Commercially Acceptable Means

Premium tax credit rules under review

  • The IRS proposed changes to who qualifies for the premium tax credit and how the credit is calculated.
  • Self-employed owners buying Exchange coverage for themselves or family should watch closely; the rules reach eligibility and credit amounts.
  • The proposal also covers liability for excess advance credit payments, which can mean a bigger bill at filing time.

Source: Federal Register: IRS: Proposed Rule: Premium Tax Credit

Ohio: no tax charged isn't tax-free

  • Ohio treats a seller's duty to collect tax separately from a buyer's duty to pay it.
  • A wine order with no Ohio tax on the receipt can still leave the buyer owing it.
  • Exemptions and credits can reduce the amount due, so check before assuming nothing is owed.

Source: CPA Practice Advisor: No Ohio Tax on Your Wine Order? You Could Still Owe It

Retirement distributions must be reported

  • The IRS is rejecting claims that IRA and qualified plan recipients didn't know they had to report distributions.
  • Owners and freelancers drawing on retirement accounts should report every distribution to avoid disputes with the IRS.

Source: CPA Practice Advisor: Don’t Try to Skip Reporting on Retirement Distributions
