Fall-risk tests explained: the CDC's chair stand, balance and Timed Up and Go

Clinicians use three short tests to screen older adults for fall risk. They come from the CDC's STEADI program. This page explains each test and the cutoff the CDC form uses, in plain English.

Where these tests come from

STEADI stands for "Stopping Elderly Accidents, Deaths & Injuries". It is a program of the CDC (the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention) that gives clinicians tools to screen, assess and help older patients who may fall. Among those tools are one-page assessment forms, dated 2017, for three tests:

  • the 30-Second Chair Stand, for leg strength and endurance;
  • The 4-Stage Balance Test, for standing (static) balance;
  • the Timed Up & Go (TUG), for mobility.

Each test needs only a stopwatch, and the chair stand and TUG need a chair. Each form ends with a plain rule for what counts as a result that points to a risk of falling. The test steps, scoring rules and cutoffs below are taken from those three forms.

Safety first: a helper stands close

All three forms tell the person running the test to stay right next to the person being tested. The chair stand form says to stand next to the patient for safety. The balance form says to stand next to the patient, hold their arm while they get into each position, and stay ready to help if they lose their balance. The TUG form says to always stay by the patient for safety.

At home, Footsure's safety checklist turns that into a few rules: a second adult stands close by the whole time; use a sturdy chair pushed against a wall so it can't slide; clear the floor of rugs, cords and pets; and stop at once if the person feels dizzy, has pain or becomes unsteady. If a doctor or therapist has said to avoid tests like these, follow that advice.

Test 1: the 30-second chair stand

Purpose on the form: to test leg strength and endurance. Equipment: a chair with a straight back and no arm rests (seat 17 inches high) and a stopwatch.

  1. Sit in the middle of the chair, feet flat on the floor, back straight.
  2. Cross the hands to the opposite shoulders, at the wrists, and keep the arms against the chest.
  3. On "Go", stand all the way up, then sit back down. Repeat for 30 seconds.

Scoring: count the number of times the person comes to a full standing position in 30 seconds. If they are more than halfway up when the 30 seconds end, it counts as a stand. If they must use their arms to stand, the form says to stop the test and record 0.

The form lists "below average" scores by age and sex. A below average score, it says, indicates a risk for falls:

Test 2: the 4-stage balance test

Purpose on the form: to assess static balance. Equipment: a stopwatch. The person tries four standing positions, each harder than the last, and holds each one for 10 seconds:

  1. Feet side by side.
  2. The instep of one foot touching the big toe of the other foot.
  3. Tandem stand: one foot in front of the other, heel touching toe.
  4. Stand on one foot.

The tester shows each position first, helps the person into it, then lets go and starts timing. The person may hold their arms out or move their body to keep their balance, but must not move their feet. If they hold a position for 10 seconds without moving their feet or needing support, they go on to the next one; if not, the test stops. The form says not to use a cane or walker, and to keep the eyes open.

The rule on the form: an older adult who cannot hold the tandem stand (position 3) for at least 10 seconds is at increased risk of falling. For those patients, the form suggests clinicians consider physical therapy for gait and balance exercises, or an evidence-based program such as Tai Chi.

Test 3: Timed Up and Go (TUG)

Purpose on the form: to assess mobility. Equipment: a stopwatch. The person wears their regular shoes and may use their usual walking aid. They start sitting back in a standard armchair, with a line on the floor 3 meters (10 feet) away.

  1. On "Go", the tester starts timing.
  2. The person stands up, walks to the line at a normal pace, turns, walks back to the chair at a normal pace and sits down.
  3. The tester stops timing when the person sits back down, and records the time in seconds.

The rule on the form: an older adult who takes 12 seconds or more to complete the TUG is at risk for falling. The form also asks the tester to watch how the person moves, for example a slow, tentative pace, loss of balance, short strides, little or no arm swing, steadying themselves on walls, shuffling, turning the whole body at once, or not using a walking aid properly, because such changes may call for further evaluation by a clinician.

What a result means, and what it doesn't

These are screening tests. A result outside the CDC cutoff is a reason to talk to a doctor or physical therapist, not a verdict. A result inside the cutoffs doesn't mean a person can't fall. A home check is not a medical diagnosis, and it doesn't replace an assessment by a clinician, who will look at much more than three tests.

The most useful thing a home check can do is start a conversation, and show change over time. Write down the numbers and the date, and bring them to the next appointment.
