Auto-renewal clauses: how to find your cancel-by date and not miss it

Many business contracts renew for another full term unless you send notice a set number of days before the current term ends. Miss that window by a day and you can be locked in for another year. Here is how to find the deadline and keep it.

What an auto-renewal clause looks like

An automatic renewal (or "evergreen") clause says the agreement continues for another term unless one side says otherwise in time. Typical wording looks like this:

"This Agreement will automatically renew for successive twelve (12) month terms unless either party gives written notice of non-renewal at least sixty (60) days before the end of the then-current term."

Three numbers matter: the end date of the current term, the length of the renewal term (sometimes the same as the first term, sometimes shorter), and the notice period. A fourth detail matters almost as much: how notice must be given.

Where to look in the contract

The renewal rule is rarely where you expect it. Check all of these:

  • The order form or quote you signed. SaaS and service deals often put the "subscription term", start date and renewal wording here, not in the main terms.
  • "Term and Termination" or "Term" in the master agreement or terms of service.
  • "Notices". This section says how notice must be sent (email, a specific address, certified mail, through the account portal) and when it counts as received.
  • "Fees" or "Pricing". Renewal price changes are often here (see the price increase page).
  • An "order of precedence" clause. If the order form and the main terms disagree, this says which wins.
  • Online terms incorporated by link. If the contract says it includes terms "available at" a web address, those terms are part of the deal and can change. Save a copy (print to PDF) on the day you sign and again before each renewal.

Search the PDF for these words: renew, automatic, successive, non-renewal, evergreen, prior to, days before, term, notice, expiration.

Work out the cancel-by date

Count backwards from the end of the current term, using the contract's own words.

Worked example
Current term ends March 31, 2027. Notice must be given "at least 60 days before the end of the then-current term."
60 calendar days before March 31, 2027 is January 30, 2027. That is the last possible day, if notice counts on the day it is sent. Treat January 23 as your real deadline so there is a week of margin, and set reminders at 90, 60 and 30 days before that.

Before you trust the date, check these questions in the contract:

  • Sent or received? If notice is effective "upon receipt", mail time counts against you. Send earlier.
  • Calendar or business days? Count calendar days unless the clause says "business days", and if the wording is unclear, use whichever reading gives the earlier date.
  • When did the term really start? The start date may be the signature date, the go-live date or the first invoice date. Check the order form, then the first invoice.
  • Has it already renewed? If a renewal already happened, the current term ends a full renewal term later. Work from the latest renewal invoice.
  • Multiple orders? Each order form or add-on may have its own term. Some contracts align them ("co-terminus"); many do not.

Put it on a calendar that someone owns

  1. Create one calendar event per contract on the cancel-by date (with your safety margin), not the renewal date.
  2. Add reminders 90, 60 and 30 days before it. Ninety days gives time to compare alternatives and negotiate; thirty days is the last call.
  3. In the event notes, paste: vendor, contract or account number, the exact notice clause, the notice method and address, the current annual cost, and a link to the signed PDF.
  4. Give each event an owner by name, and invite a second person so a holiday or departure does not sink it.
  5. Keep a simple list (a spreadsheet is fine) with one row per contract: vendor, cost per year, term end, notice days, cancel-by date, notice method, owner, decision.

Sample non-renewal notice

Send it by the method the Notices section requires, and keep proof (a sent email with the vendor's reply, a portal confirmation screenshot, or a mail receipt). Adapt this wording:

Subject: Notice of non-renewal: [Company] account [number]

To: [Vendor legal name], [notice address or email from the contract]

This is written notice under section [X] of the [agreement name]
dated [date] that [Your company legal name] does not wish to renew
the agreement (including order form [number]) when the current term
ends on [end date]. Please do not renew any part of the subscription
and do not charge us for any period after [end date].

Please confirm in writing that you received this notice and that the
agreement will end on [end date].

[Name], [Title]
[Company], [date]

Non-renewal is not the same as termination for cause, and you do not need to give a reason. If you still want to talk about renewing on better terms, you can say so in a separate message: sending notice first keeps your options open while you negotiate.

Is there a law that protects my business?

Usually the contract governs, so do not count on a law to rescue a missed window. On the federal side, the FTC's negative option rule in the eCFR, 16 CFR Part 425, now covers "prenotification negative option plans" for goods: the model where a seller sends announcements of upcoming selections and ships them unless the member declines. The eCFR history shows that a broader version, which included a "Simple cancellation (Click to Cancel)" section, was in effect from January 14, 2025 and removed on February 12, 2026. State laws on automatic renewal vary; many are written for consumer subscriptions, and we could not open the state statutes while writing this page. Ask a lawyer in your state if a renewal you missed is large.
