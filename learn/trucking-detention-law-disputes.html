Can a carrier claim detention pay without written authorization?

A carrier should not assume detention pay is owed when the rate confirmation and incorporated broker-carrier agreement contain no authorization; review the full contract and request written approval before relying on a claim.

Why a silent rate confirmation is not an automatic payment promise

FMCSA's 2024 detention research notice says there is no standard federal definition of detention time. It describes the two-hour threshold as a convention used in industry, government and research to measure facility dwell time. That measurement convention is not a federal rule requiring a broker to pay every carrier after two hours.

When the rate confirmation is silent, check the complete agreement before deciding what is owed. The load confirmation may incorporate a broker-carrier agreement, accessorial schedule, tariff, facility policy or other terms. A written amendment or later message from an authorized broker representative may also matter. The wording, timing, authority of the person who approved it and governing law can change the analysis. A wait by itself does not establish an agreed detention rate.

Do not treat a verbal assurance such as “we will take care of you” as a substitute for written approval. Ask the broker to confirm the terms in the rate confirmation or a written amendment, and keep the reply with the load file.

Check these records before making a claim

  • Signed rate confirmation and every revision. Check the detention clause, free-time trigger, hourly rate, billing increments, cap, notice deadline, required approval and proof rules. Keep the version that was in force when the load was accepted.
  • Broker-carrier agreement and incorporated schedules. Look for accessorial definitions, pre-approval requirements, shipper-payment conditions, waiver language, notice deadlines, setoff rights, governing-law clauses and dispute procedures.
  • Appointment and dispatch records. Save the tender, appointment time, facility address, check-in instructions and any change sent by the broker or shipper.
  • Arrival and departure evidence. Keep gate receipts, signed bills of lading, receiver in/out stamps, dock paperwork, ELD or GPS timestamps and other records that show when the truck arrived and was released.
  • Notice and approval messages. Preserve emails, texts, app messages and call notes showing when you notified the broker, whether the broker asked for more information and whether an authorized person approved a charge.
  • Settlement and payment records. Keep the broker's settlement or remittance detail, any deduction explanation, the invoice and the bank or factoring statement. Separate a factor's fee from a broker settlement variance.

What to do before free time expires

Read the notice procedure before the truck reaches the facility. If the contract requires a call, tracking status or written request before free time expires, follow that step and save proof that you did. Send a timestamped message with the load number, facility, arrival time and expected delay. Ask the broker to approve the charge in writing before the truck is released.

Ask for the terms to be specific: when free time begins, how many hours are free, the hourly rate, whether the charge is paid in 15-minute or full-hour increments, any cap, what proof is required, who can approve it and when the request must be submitted. If the broker changes the terms, request a revised rate confirmation or a written amendment that identifies the load.

What to do after the delay

If no written authorization exists, do not state that payment is guaranteed. Submit a prompt, factual request for an exception or post-load adjustment. Include the timeline, the relevant contract language, the amount requested and copies of the supporting records. Ask the broker to identify the specific clause or missing document if it denies the request. Do not calculate a claim using an assumed industry rate when the contract does not provide one.

If the settlement is lower than the agreed load amount, compare the signed rate confirmation, written amendments and approved accessorials with the broker's settlement line by line. Ask for a written explanation of each deduction. Keep factoring fees, reserves and advances on a separate reconciliation track.

What federal broker records rules do—and do not—say

Under 49 CFR 371.3, brokers must keep a record of each transaction for three years, including specified information about the transaction, broker compensation and freight charges collected and paid. Each party to a brokered transaction has a right to review the record required by that section. A carrier can identify the load and request the transaction record in writing.

That record-review rule does not set a detention hourly rate and does not, by itself, create a contractual promise to pay an unapproved accessorial. Read the exact regulation and your contract together. If the amount is material or the broker disputes the contract, get advice from a qualified attorney in the relevant jurisdiction.

Hours of service and broker financial responsibility are separate issues

Federal hours-of-service rules treat certain waiting time at a shipper or receiver as on-duty time under 49 CFR 395.2, and 49 CFR 395.3 sets driving and on-duty limits. Those safety rules matter when planning the next leg, but they do not set a broker's detention price.

FMCSA's broker financial-responsibility requirements took effect January 16, 2026. The agency describes the $75,000 financial-security requirement and the process that can lead to suspension if available security falls below that amount and is not replenished after notice. Do not treat that separate financial-responsibility rule as automatic approval of a particular detention charge or proof that a specific claim is covered.

Questions carriers should ask

Does two hours automatically trigger detention pay?

No. FMCSA's 2024 research notice describes two hours as a commonly used measurement convention for dwell time, not a universal federal payment threshold. Check the written terms that govern the load.

Can I use 49 CFR 371.3 to demand a detention payment?

The rule gives each party a right to review the broker's required transaction record. It does not establish the detention rate or prove that the broker approved an accessorial. Use it to request the record, then compare the record with the contract and settlement.

What if the broker approved detention by text?

Preserve the full message, sender, timestamp and load reference. Whether it changes the agreement depends on the contract, the sender's authority and applicable law. Do not assume every message is a binding amendment.
