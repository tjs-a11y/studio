Factoring fee or broker short-pay? Telling two deductions apart

A factoring company’s fee comes out of your advance. A broker short-pay is the broker paying less than the rate confirmation said. On a bank statement they look like the same missing money — but they are two separate gaps, with two separate paper trails and two separate fixes.

Two different gaps that look the same

When a deposit lands smaller than you expected, there are usually two very different reasons, and they are easy to mix up:

  • A factoring deduction. If you factor your invoices, your factoring company advances most of the invoice up front and keeps a fee. That gap is between you and your factor, and it is set by your factoring agreement.
  • A broker short-pay. The broker pays less than the amount on the rate confirmation — often with a reason attached (a lumper that was not reimbursed, a late fee, a claim, a detention request that was denied). That gap is between you and the broker, and it is set by the load paperwork.

On a bank statement, both just look like “less money than the load was worth.” Telling which one happened — and whether anything is actually wrong — means pulling the documents, not guessing from the deposit.

How a factoring deduction is built

Two numbers drive it, and they are not the same thing:

  • The advance rate is how much of the invoice you get up front — trade sources report this often sits in the 90%–100% range.
  • The factoring fee (or factoring rate) is what the factor charges — commonly reported around 1%–5% of the invoice, with many established carriers landing near 2%–3.5%. The exact numbers vary by provider and contract.

A worked example the trade sources use: on a $2,000 invoice at a 90% advance and a 3% fee, you receive $1,800 up front. When the customer pays, the $200 reserve is released minus the $60 fee, so $140 more comes back. Your total is $1,940, and the $60 fee is the gap. (estimate — illustrative figures from the sources below; your own schedule may differ.)

Factors price this in one of three shapes: a flat fee, a tiered or variable fee that grows the longer the invoice stays open, or an advance-plus-reserve structure like the example. Other line items can appear too — setup, transaction, monthly minimum, termination, and non-ACH payment fees. And the agreement is either recourse (you buy back invoices the customer never pays; usually the cheaper option, because you keep the credit risk) or non-recourse (the factor absorbs some losses, often only when a broker or shipper goes bankrupt; usually costs more). Ask for a sample settlement statement at 30, 45 and 60 days before you sign.

What a broker short-pay looks like

A short-pay is the broker remitting less than the rate confirmation amount. Sometimes it is legitimate (a deduction you agreed to, a documented claim); sometimes it is an error or a disputed charge. The only way to know is to compare the rate confirmation and the broker’s settlement line by line, and to read the reason attached to the deduction.

Federal rules require brokers to keep a record of each transaction, and a party to the transaction can request it; driver advocates such as OOIDA publish a broker-document request form for exactly this. In practice that right is contested — many carrier contracts try to waive it, and broker transparency is the subject of ongoing FMCSA rulemaking — so check the current official source before you rely on it.

The documents to pull before you decide

You cannot reconcile a short deposit from memory. Gather these and lay them side by side:

  • The rate confirmation and any revised versions.
  • Proof of delivery, and any accessorial approval with timestamps (detention in/out, lumper receipt, layover).
  • The broker settlement or remittance detail — the line items and the reason for any deduction.
  • Your factoring agreement and its fee schedule.
  • The factor’s reserve and chargeback ledger and the funding statement for the invoice.
  • The bank deposit itself.

Line up agreed against paid on two tracks — the broker settlement against the rate confirmation, and the factoring funding against your agreement — and the gap lands in one track or the other, not a vague total.

Why you keep the two apart

A factoring fee is not a broker short-pay, and vice versa. Mix them and you can chase a broker over money your own factor kept, or write off a real short-pay as “just the factoring fee.” Keep two ledgers:

  • Load vs broker settlement — did the broker pay the rate confirmation amount, and is any deduction documented?
  • Invoice vs factoring deposit — did the factor advance and settle according to your agreement?

A gap in either ledger is a flag to pull the paperwork, not proof that anyone did anything wrong. This page is a plain-English explainer, not legal, financial or accounting advice.
