The average U.S. electricity price in 2026 — and how to read your own

If you want to know whether your electricity rate is high, you need a yardstick. The U.S. average residential price in 2026 is roughly 18 cents per kilowatt-hour — but the number comes with caveats, and your own rate is the one that matters.

The number

For 2026, the U.S. average residential electricity price is in the neighbourhood of 18 cents per kilowatt-hour. The Empire Center's April 2026 Energy Data Bulletin, drawing on EIA retail-sales data, put the national average at about 18.83¢/kWh in April 2026, up roughly 7.3% from 17.55¢ a year earlier. EIA's own Short-Term Energy Outlook projects the full-year 2026 residential average near the same level (an increase of a few percent over 2025). Both are national figures; your state can be far higher or lower.

What the figure does and doesn't mean

Two caveats keep this honest. First, EIA's "average price" is not a survey of what people pay per plan — it is total sales revenue divided by total kilowatt-hours, so it is an approximation, a point the Empire Center notes directly. Second, state spreads are enormous: high-cost states can run more than double the national average, while some run well below it. A national average is a yardstick for "is my rate roughly normal," not a target you should expect to hit.

How to read your own rate

Your real rate is simple to compute from a single bill: divide the total dollars for energy by the kilowatt-hours used that month. A $150 charge for 1,000 kWh is $0.15, or 15¢/kWh. Comparing that to the ~18¢ national figure tells you whether you are below, around, or above average — and if you are well above it, the most common reason is a plan that has rolled off its fixed rate onto a variable default (see the companion page on what happens when a fixed-rate plan ends).

Why bills have been rising

The year-over-year increase in the national average is real, not noise: the April 2026 figure was up about 7% over the prior April. Rising delivery and generation costs feed retail prices with a lag, which is why a rate that looked fine last year can be below today's offers. That lag is also why the end of a fixed plan matters so much — you can be locked in below the market, or stranded above it, depending only on timing.
