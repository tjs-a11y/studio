What happens when your fixed-rate electricity plan ends

The riskiest day in the life of an electricity plan is the day it ends. If you do nothing, you are not cut off — you are moved onto a month-to-month rate that is usually higher than the deal you had. Here is how that works and how to stay ahead of it.

The short answer

A fixed-rate plan locks your price per kilowatt-hour for a set term — often 12, 24 or 36 months. When that term ends and you have not signed a new plan or switched provider, your service does not stop. Instead, in deregulated retail markets, the provider rolls you onto a month-to-month, variable "default" plan, and that rate is typically higher than the fixed deal you just left.

What the rules say

The Public Utility Commission of Texas consumer page describes the two plan types and the end-of-term behaviour plainly: a variable-rate plan has no fixed term or cancellation fee, but the rate per kilowatt-hour "can vary from month to month," moving up or down with the market and the provider. The same consumer guidance is clear that if your plan ends and you have not chosen a new one, your provider keeps serving you — on that month-to-month plan — rather than disconnecting you. Texas is the clearest example because its retail market is fully open, but the same renewal pattern appears across deregulated markets.

Why the roll-off usually costs more

Two things push the default rate up. First, a variable rate is not protected by a contract, so it tracks wholesale costs and the provider's discretion — and providers have little reason to make the "do nothing" rate their cheapest. Second, renewal offers sent to existing customers are often priced above the rates advertised to brand-new customers. The result is that the households least likely to shop — the ones who let a plan lapse — tend to pay the most.

How to get ahead of it

The fix is a date, not a decision made under pressure. Find your plan's end date (it is on your contract or "facts" document), and mark a reminder 60 to 90 days before it. That window is enough to compare offers and re-sign or switch before the roll-off rate ever applies. On a month-to-month plan there is generally no cancellation fee, so leaving is cheap once you are already on it — the cost is the weeks or months you spend on the higher rate before you notice.
