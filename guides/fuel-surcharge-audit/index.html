Fuel surcharge audit: check the rate, index and miles

Updated 9 November 2026 · Carrier payment checklist

A fuel surcharge is not one universal government rate. It is a pricing term negotiated between the parties. To audit it, find the exact formula in the contract or rate confirmation, then verify the inputs the formula actually uses: index, week, base price, mileage and eligible miles.

Start with the written formula—not a generic online calculator

Collect the master transportation agreement, current rate confirmation, fuel-surcharge schedule and any amendments. Look for:

  • Reference index: EIA U.S. average or a regional diesel series, or another named source.
  • Trigger/base price: the diesel price at which the surcharge begins, and whether the base is fixed or periodically reset.
  • Adjustment step: the cents-per-gallon or cents-per-mile increment for each price band.
  • Effective date: which publication week applies to the shipment or invoice.
  • Mileage basis: practical miles, dispatched miles, loaded miles, round trip or a named routing source.
  • Payment basis: cents per mile, percentage of linehaul, flat amount or another formula.

Verify the index and the effective week

The U.S. Energy Information Administration publishes weekly retail on-highway diesel prices. EIA explains that transportation companies may use these prices in their formulas, but EIA does not calculate, assess or regulate the surcharge and does not prescribe one formula. Your agreement determines which series and week apply.

Open the source series named in your contract. Record the publication date, geographic region and price. Do not substitute the current national price for a regional series or a historical week if the contract specifies something else.

Recalculate using the contract's steps

Suppose a contract says the surcharge is $0.01 per mile for each $0.05 per gallon above a $1.50 base, and the applicable index is $3.00. The increase is $1.50, or 30 increments of $0.05. The illustrative surcharge is $0.30 per eligible mile. At 600 eligible miles, that is $180.

This example is arithmetic only, not a recommended industry formula. Real contracts may use different breakpoints, rounding, regions, base prices and miles. If the agreement uses a percentage of linehaul, do not apply a cents-per-mile formula.

Audit the invoice inputs

  1. Match the load number and dates to the rate confirmation.
  2. Confirm the correct EIA region or other contracted index and publication week.
  3. Recompute the surcharge from the stated base and adjustment table.
  4. Check the mileage source and confirm the correct miles were included.
  5. Check rounding, caps, minimums and whether fuel is already included in an all-in rate.
  6. Compare your result with the surcharge line on the remittance and record the variance.

What to send if the amount does not match

Send a short reconciliation with the contract formula, source series and week, calculation, mileage source, amount expected and amount paid. Ask which input or contract provision explains the difference. Keep the source page or downloaded data snapshot with the load file. Avoid claiming that an EIA number alone establishes what a broker owes; the negotiated formula is essential.
