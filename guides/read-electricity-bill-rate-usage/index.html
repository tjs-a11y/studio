How to read an electricity bill before forecasting the next 12 months

Reviewed October 31, 2026 · Sources listed below

To make a useful bill estimate, find the billing-period kilowatt-hours, the price or rate that applies to that usage, the plan end date, and any separate delivery, fixed or tax charges. Do not assume the largest number on the bill is the per-kilowatt-hour rate: the total may combine energy, delivery, base fees, taxes, credits and other adjustments.

Find these items on the bill

  • Billing period. Note the start and end dates. A 35-day bill should not be compared directly with a 28-day bill without accounting for the different number of days.
  • Usage in kWh. This is the amount of electricity billed for the period. Keep the usage and the billing dates together.
  • Energy or supply rate. Look for the cents-per-kWh price, or use the plan document to identify the rate that applies to your usage band.
  • Delivery or distribution charges. These may be listed separately from the energy charge and may include a per-kWh amount or a fixed monthly amount.
  • Fixed charges, taxes, credits and adjustments. Note recurring base fees, taxes, credits, late charges and one-time adjustments separately. They can change the total without changing the advertised energy rate.
  • Plan end date and renewal terms. Check the contract or electricity facts label, not only the bill. A bill may not repeat every term of the plan.

Separate a plan rate from an all-in average

If you divide the total bill by the kWh used, you get an all-in average cost per kWh for that billing period. That can be a useful household budgeting yardstick, but it is not necessarily the energy supply rate in your contract. A fixed monthly charge, taxes or a one-time credit can make that average look higher or lower. For comparing competing plans, read the plan terms and the applicable electricity facts label or tariff, and compare the same usage level and billing assumptions.

The Public Utility Commission of Texas explains the difference between fixed and variable electricity plans and directs consumers to review plan details. That Texas guidance is useful in a competitive retail market; rules, bill layouts and switching arrangements differ in other states. Always use the documents and regulator for your own jurisdiction.

What Bill Radar can and cannot estimate

Bill Radar uses the rate, usage and plan end date you enter to build an adjustable 12-month estimate. It does not connect to your utility, retrieve your tariff, verify the rate on your contract or predict a future rate offer. A single rate multiplied by usage is a simplified model; separate base fees, taxes, credits, time-of-use periods and tiered pricing may not be represented as separate line items. If those charges matter, keep them visible in your own comparison and treat the tool output as an estimate, not a quote.

Before using the forecast, keep a copy of the bill and plan document beside you. Enter the units carefully, check whether the rate is cents or dollars per kWh, and adjust the assumptions rather than treating the default assumptions as your utility’s actual future price.
