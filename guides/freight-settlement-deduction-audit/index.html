Freight settlement audit: how to check carrier deductions and short pays

Updated 9 November 2026 · Carrier payment checklist

A settlement that is lower than expected is a signal to reconcile the paperwork—not proof, by itself, that a broker acted improperly. Match each payment line to the signed rate confirmation, delivery record, approved accessorials and remittance. Keep factoring activity separate so you can identify exactly where the difference occurred.

Build the load file before doing the math

Use one folder or record per load. Keep the original documents and preserve the email or message that approved any change.

  • Signed rate confirmation and amendments: record linehaul, fuel surcharge, accessorial rates, free time, notice rules, caps and any later written changes.
  • Bill of lading and proof of delivery: match the load number, pickup and delivery dates, quantities, signatures and exceptions.
  • Accessorial evidence: keep arrival and departure timestamps, ELD or tracking records, facility check-in slips, lumper receipts and written approvals.
  • Invoice and remittance advice: capture the gross amount, every deduction, adjustment, reference number and stated reason.
  • Factoring statement and bank deposit: identify advance, reserve, factoring fee and reserve release separately from the broker's gross payment.

Reconcile the expected gross amount

Start with the amount agreed in the signed rate confirmation. Add only accessorials that were earned under the written terms and for which you have the required evidence. Then compare that expected gross with the broker's remittance—not with the net bank deposit if a factor is involved.

Classify every difference instead of arguing over one total

  1. Rate mismatch: the remittance does not match the signed linehaul, fuel or amended rate.
  2. Accessorial not paid: the charge was agreed, but the broker says notice, approval or proof is missing. Check the exact condition and whether you met it.
  3. Damage, service or compliance deduction: ask for the specific load, contract clause, calculation and supporting evidence. Do not assume a generic label such as “chargeback” explains the amount.
  4. Factoring difference: reconcile gross broker remittance to the factor's statement, then reconcile the factor's net payment to the bank. Do not attribute a factor fee to the broker.
  5. Timing difference: check whether a reserve, quick-pay fee, credit memo or later adjustment is involved.

Ask for the transaction record and a line-by-line explanation

Under 49 CFR § 371.3, brokers must keep records of each transaction for three years, and each party to a brokered transaction has a right to review the record required by that section. The regulation describes specified transaction information; it does not mean every disputed amount is automatically owed or that every private contract term disappears.

Make a written request that identifies the load and asks to review the transaction record under § 371.3. Separately ask the broker to identify the contract provision and documents supporting each deduction. Keep a copy of your request and the response. If the issue remains unresolved, review the governing agreement's dispute procedure and consider qualified legal or industry advice.

A concise dispute packet

  1. One-page reconciliation: load number, expected gross, actual gross, each variance and total disputed amount.
  2. Copies of the signed rate confirmation and written amendments.
  3. BOL/POD, timestamps, receipts and approval messages tied to each accessorial.
  4. Remittance advice with disputed lines highlighted.
  5. Factoring statement on a separate page, if applicable.
  6. A clear request: identify the clause and evidence for each deduction, correct any arithmetic or payment error, and provide a written response.
