How to compare contractor and auto repair estimates

Compare estimates by matching the work scope, parts or materials, labor, schedule, total price and approval terms; ask each provider to explain major differences before choosing.

Reviewed October 31, 2026 · Sources listed below

Make the estimates comparable

  • Describe the same job. Give each contractor or repair shop the same problem statement, measurements, photos and requested outcome.
  • Compare the scope line by line. Check which tasks are included, excluded or listed as optional. A quote for a partial repair is not directly comparable to a full replacement.
  • Identify parts and materials. Ask for brand, model, grade, quantity, whether parts are new or remanufactured, and whether disposal or delivery is included.
  • Separate labor and other charges. Ask about diagnostic time, trip fees, permits, disposal, taxes, shop supplies, deposits and change-order costs.
  • Check the schedule. Compare estimated start and completion dates, availability, and what happens if materials or parts are delayed.
  • Ask about credentials. For home improvement, check the license and insurance requirements in your state or county and ask for proof of coverage.

What a written estimate should contain

The FTC advises consumers to get more than one home-improvement estimate. Its guidance says a written estimate should describe the work, materials, completion date, price and contractor contact information. It cautions against automatically choosing the lowest bid and recommends asking for an explanation when estimates differ substantially. The FTC also recommends checking a contractor’s license and insurance and reading the written contract carefully. Contract requirements and cancellation rights vary by state and by where the agreement is signed.

For auto repair, the FTC says a written estimate should identify the condition to be repaired, the parts needed and the anticipated labor charge. Ask for a signed copy and ask the shop to state that it will contact you before doing work above a specified amount or time. The FTC notes that state law may require this approval process. Ask whether there is a diagnostic fee if you decide to have the work performed elsewhere.

Questions to ask before approving

  1. What exact work is included, and what is not included?
  2. Which parts or materials are being supplied, and are there alternatives?
  3. What could cause the price to change, and will I approve changes in writing first?
  4. What warranty covers parts, labor and workmanship?
  5. What payment schedule, deposit and completion date are written into the contract?
