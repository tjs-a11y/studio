How to check an unexpected text or email for scam warning signs

Do not click a link or reply to an unexpected text or email that pressures you to fix an account, pay a fee or share personal information; verify the claim through a website or phone number you already know is real.

Reviewed October 31, 2026 · Sources listed below

Warning signs worth checking

  • Unexpected account or fraud alert. The message claims a bank, retailer or service found a problem and asks you to call a number or confirm information through a link.
  • Delivery, toll or fee demand. It says a package is delayed, a toll is unpaid or a small fee is needed before something can be released.
  • Urgency or pressure. It threatens account closure, a fine or loss of access unless you act immediately.
  • Requests for sensitive information. It asks for a password, one-time code, Social Security number, card number or bank details.
  • Unfamiliar links or attachments. The displayed company name does not match the actual domain, or the message asks you to open an unexpected attachment.

One warning sign does not prove who sent the message, and a polished message can still be fraudulent. The safest response is to stop using the contact details in the message. Open the company’s official app or type its known web address yourself, or call the number printed on a card or statement.

What to do with a suspicious text

  1. Do not reply, click, download an attachment or call the number in the message.
  2. Contact the named organization through a known website, app or phone number and ask whether the notice is genuine.
  3. Use your phone’s report-junk option or forward unwanted texts to 7726 (SPAM), where supported.
  4. Report fraud attempts to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
  5. If you shared a password, change it through the official service and review account security. If you shared bank or card details, contact the financial institution using a trusted number.

The FTC warns about unexpected texts that impersonate banks, delivery services and toll agencies. It recommends not replying or clicking, checking with the real organization using contact details you already trust, and reporting unwanted texts. The FTC also explains that phishing messages may use familiar brands to prompt people to disclose sensitive information.
